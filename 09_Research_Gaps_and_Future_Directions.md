# 09 — Research Gaps and Future Directions

## 1. Materials design
- Improve lead-free compositions without optimizing one coefficient at the expense of loss, reliability, temperature stability, or manufacturability.
- Clarify links among phase composition, texture, domain structure, defects, grain boundaries, and electromechanical response.
- Develop reproducible flexible composites with controlled filler dispersion, interface chemistry, and fatigue performance.
- Report environmental and end-of-life impacts alongside performance.

## 2. Energy harvesting under real conditions
Research question: how robust is a device when excitation frequency and amplitude vary realistically? Map output over a frequency–amplitude matrix, measure load-dependent power, quantify rectifier/storage losses, and validate under representative field excitation. Compare resonant and broadband designs using the same energy budget.

## 3. Flexible sensors and generalization
Research question: does sensitivity remain stable across devices, users, temperature, humidity, and repeated bending? Combine reference-sensor calibration, cycling, environmental tests, and independent-device validation. For machine learning, split data by participant or device when generalization to unseen users/devices is the goal.

## 4. Piezocatalysis and energy accounting
Research question: does pollutant removal result from piezoelectric charge effects, adsorption, heating, direct ultrasound chemistry, or their combination? Use factorial controls for light, excitation, and catalyst; calibrated mechanical/acoustic input; adsorption equilibrium; product/mineralization measurements; and cycle/leaching tests. Report energy cost per treated volume or pollutant mass when feasible.

## 5. LDH and LDH-derived materials
LDHs are compositionally tunable layered hydroxides, but piezoelectric behavior must not be assumed from layered structure alone. Establish phase and symmetry, directly test electromechanical response, and rule out artifacts from contact electrification, ionic conduction, or measurement geometry.

Possible comparative design:
1. Characterize phase, composition, morphology, and electrical properties.
2. Measure piezoelectric response with calibrated methods and controls.
3. Compare pristine LDH, thermally treated derivative, known piezoelectric phase, and physical mixtures.
4. Standardize catalyst loading, pollutant concentration, pH, mechanical input, light, and reactor geometry.
5. Analyze intermediates, mineralization, leaching, and reuse.

## 6. Biomedical translation
Separate material-mediated effects from mechanical, electrical, chemical, and thermal effects. Test under hydrated, physiologically relevant conditions. Assess sterilization, fatigue, leaching, inflammation, and long-term stability. Do not extrapolate cell viability alone to clinical benefit.

## 7. Data standards and open science
A shared dataset should include composition, synthesis, phase/poling, dimensions, density, electrodes, test mode, frequency, temperature, load, calibration, uncertainty, and raw waveforms. Machine-readable metadata improves comparability and supports physics-informed machine learning.

## 8. High-value project concepts
1. Benchmark three material families under identical excitation and load conditions.
2. Relate flexible-composite filler dispersion and interface chemistry to response, fatigue, and signal-to-noise ratio.
3. Quantify mechanical excitation, light, adsorption, and catalyst contributions in piezocatalysis.
4. Measure lead-free material performance after cycling, humidity exposure, and thermal aging.
5. Build an open dataset that records uncertainty and experimental context, not only headline coefficients.

## From gap to hypothesis
State (a) the unresolved mechanism or limitation, (b) a measurable independent variable, (c) a quantitative outcome, (d) controls, and (e) a falsifiable expected result. “Improve piezoelectric performance” is a goal, not yet a testable hypothesis.
